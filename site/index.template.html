The Timber­werewolves

Do the Minnesota Timberwolves play better under a full moon?

{{moon_1}}The record

Over the last ten seasons the Timberwolves are {{near_record}} in games played within a day and a half of a full moon. In every other game they are {{far_record}}. That is a {{near_pct}}% team under a full moon and a {{far_pct}}% team the rest of the month.

I tested that gap with {{games}} NBA games, the position of the moon at every tip-off, and a model that compares the Wolves with the other 29 teams.

Once the model accounts for opponents, home court and rest, the Wolves' point margin is {{window_net_plain}} points better near a full moon, with a 95% interval from {{window_net_lo}} to {{window_net_hi}}. The moon shows no effect on their scoring. Fake moons, cycles of made-up lengths, produce a record as good as {{near_record}} about {{fake_share}}% of the time. In the {{second_seasons}} seasons before these ten, the Wolves won {{second_near_pct}}% of their games near a full moon and {{second_far_pct}}% of the rest.

{{moon_2}}The games

The scores come from ESPN by way of the sportsdataverse project, which publishes one schedule file per NBA season. I used the ten seasons from {{first_season}} through {{last_season}}: {{games}} completed games, {{wolves_games}} of them Wolves games, counting the regular season, the play-in and the playoffs. The {{next_season}} season was under way when I ran this, and I left it out. I pinned the files to one commit of the source repository, so every run reads the same bytes.

Four things needed cleaning before any modeling.

  • All-Star exhibitions are out. They use one-off teams such as Team LeBron, and {{exhibitions}} of them were in the files.
  • {{time_fixed}} games from February 2020 carry tip-off times about ten hours late, before 11 a.m. Eastern. I moved them back twelve hours so each sits on the date it was played.
  • {{neutral_games}} games count as neutral sites with no home-court advantage: the 2020 Orlando bubble, the NBA Cup games in Las Vegas and the games played abroad.
  • A team's days of rest come from its previous game in the same season. Zero means the second night of a back-to-back.

Those ten seasons hold {{lunar_cycles}} lunar months with games in them.

{{moon_3}}The moon

The R package suncalc gives the moon's illuminated fraction, its phase and its distance from Earth at any instant. I computed all three at each tip-off. Checked against PyEphem, a high-precision ephemeris, suncalc's illuminated fraction correlates {{frac_cor}} over these ten years, which is plenty for something that takes {{synodic_days}} days to cycle.

The exact instant of each full moon is the one thing I took from PyEphem directly. suncalc's full-moon times run up to {{max_hours_off}} hours off, and a window with a hard edge at a day and a half should not move by a few hours.

Phase is a position on a circle. The night before a new moon and the night after it are neighbors, yet one has phase 0.98 and the other 0.02. A regression given phase as a plain number would treat those two nights as opposites. Cosine and sine fix that, because together they describe a position on a circle with two ordinary numbers.

Four variables go into the models.

moon_cos
+1 at full moon and −1 at new moon. It is the illuminated fraction rescaled, so it measures how full the moon is.
moon_sin
−1 at first quarter and +1 at last quarter. It tells the nights before a full moon from the nights after. With both terms in a model, a monthly rhythm that peaks anywhere in the cycle shows up in one or the other.
near_full
1 when tip-off is within {{near_full_days}} days of the full-moon instant, the three nights the moon looks full, and 0 otherwise. I set that width in the first script, before any results existed.
moon_dist
Distance from Earth in thousands of kilometers. It runs on its own {{anomalistic_days}}-day cycle and is the supermoon variable. suncalc's distance is the roughest of its three outputs, correlating {{dist_cor}} with PyEphem.

The first versions of this analysis used illuminated fraction and days from the full moon as separate variables. Their correlation was {{frac_days_cor}}, which makes them one variable entered twice, and a model cannot tell two copies of one thing apart.

{{moon_4}}The model

Comparing full-moon games with all the others is not a fair fight. The Wolves played {{near_home_pct}}% of their full-moon games at home and {{far_home_pct}}% of the rest. In 2021-22, when they went {{s22_record}} under full moons, {{s22_home}} of those {{s22_games}} games were in Minneapolis.

So the model starts by predicting every team's score in every game from things that have nothing to do with the moon:

  • the season, since league scoring rose about {{season_rise}} points per team over the decade
  • home court, estimated separately for each season
  • days of rest for both teams
  • whether the game is a play-in or playoff game
  • overtime periods
  • day of the week

It also learns how good each offense and each defense was in each season, which teams ran hot or cold in a given month, how league scoring drifted from month to month, and how fast each individual game was played. In lme4 those are random intercepts.

Before the moon enters, the model's estimates look like basketball. Home court is worth about {{home_typical}} points and fell to {{home_2021}} in 2020-21. A team with one day of rest scores {{rest1}} more than a team on the second night of a back-to-back. Playoff games run {{playoff_drop}} points lower per team, and each overtime period adds {{overtime_pts}}.

Then each moon variable goes in three times: once for the whole league, once for the Wolves when they have the ball, and once for the Wolves on defense. The league term is there because the other 29 teams played under the same moon. If scoring dips everywhere on full-moon nights, the Wolves get no credit for it. What remains is how the Wolves differ from everyone else.

points ~ moon + moon:is_wolves + moon:vs_wolves
       + is_wolves + vs_wolves + season + season:home_court
       + rest + opp_rest + game_type + overtimes + dow
       + (1 | season_month) + (1 | team_season) + (1 | opp_season)
       + (1 | team_month)   + (1 | opp_month)   + (1 | game_id)

The model has {{team_games}} rows, two per game, one for each team's score.

{{moon_5}}The result

The figure holds sixteen estimates: four moon variables, each measured for the league, for the Wolves' scoring, for the Wolves' points allowed and for the Wolves' margin.

The moon does nothing visible to the Wolves' scoring. From new moon to full, it changes by {{off_cos}} points relative to the league, with an interval from {{off_cos_lo}} to {{off_cos_hi}}.

Defense is where {{near_record}} comes from. Inside the full-moon window, opponents score {{def_win_abs}} fewer points against the Wolves than the model expects (p = {{def_win_p}}).

Scoring and defense together give the margin. The Wolves are {{window_net_plain}} points better inside the window, with an interval from {{window_net_lo}} to {{window_net_hi}}. Measured the smooth way, from new moon to full moon, the edge is {{cos_net}} points ({{cos_net_lo}} to {{cos_net_hi}}).

The other small p-value has nothing to do with Minnesota. Across the league, teams score {{dist_league_abs}} fewer points when the moon is at its farthest than when it is nearest (p = {{dist_league_p}}).

Two estimates out of sixteen under 0.05 is about what luck produces. With no moon effect at all I would expect {{expected_flags}} of them, and two or more would show up roughly one time in five. Holm's correction, applied within each group of four, moves those two p-values to {{dist_league_holm}} and {{def_win_holm}}. One test of all eight Wolves scoring and defense terms together gives p = {{omni_wolves_p}}. The same test of the four league-wide terms gives p = {{omni_league_p}}, which is the distance effect again.

Split the month into its eight named phases and the Wolves are {{full_phase}} points better than expected at the full moon and {{new_phase}} better at the new moon. If moonlight were the cause, those two phases should sit at opposite ends.

The simpler analysis uses only the Wolves' own {{wolves_games}} games and tries every combination of the four moon variables, sixteen models per outcome, ranked by AIC. For points scored and for margin, the best model contains no moon variable. For points allowed and for wins, the best model adds the full-moon window and nothing else. Alone, the window's p-value is {{wo_allowed_p}} for points allowed and {{wo_win_p}} for wins, or {{wo_allowed_holm}} and {{wo_win_holm}} after Holm's correction for the four variables. Of the four moon variables the window is the best predictor, and what it predicts is defense.

{{moon_6}}Fake moons

Every p-value above depends on the model's error bars, and basketball gives reasons to doubt them. Teams run hot and cold for weeks. A variable that rises and falls once a month could ride those streaks and look meaningful.

So I invented moons. A fake moon is a cycle of some other length, anywhere from {{fake_min_days}} to {{fake_max_days}} days. There are {{fake_moons}} of them here, spaced so that each is a separate test. None of them exists, so whatever effect the model finds for one is noise, and the whole set shows how much noise to expect.

The first test uses the raw record a fan would quote. On every fake cycle I marked a window covering {{window_share}}% of the cycle, the same share the real full-moon window takes of a lunar month. I placed it at four points in the cycle and counted the Wolves' wins and losses inside. Of {{fake_windows}} fake windows, {{fake_share}}% hold a record as good as {{near_record}}. The best belongs to a cycle of {{best_fake_days}} days, on which the Wolves went {{best_fake_record}}.

Every fake moon also went through the same model as the real one, twice, starting at two different points in its cycle. For the Wolves' margin, the fake estimates have a standard deviation of {{fake_sd_net}} points and the model's standard error is {{model_se_net}}, so the error bars are the right size. The real moon lands in the middle of the cloud. Of the fake estimates, {{p_fake_net}}% are larger.

One term does worse. For points allowed, the fake estimates spread {{def_cos_excess}}% wider than the model's standard error allows when measured from new moon to full, and {{def_excess_pct}}% wider for the window, so the {{def_win_p}} above is too small. Measured against the fake windows it is {{def_win_p_fake}}.

The test can also say yes. I added a {{inj_swing}}-point swing to the Wolves' real scores, timed to the real moon, and ran it again. The estimate for margin moved to {{inj_net}}, beyond {{inj_beyond}} of the {{fake_estimates}} fake estimates.

{{moon_7}}What ten seasons cannot see

This analysis would catch a {{detect_net}}-point swing in the Wolves' margin between new and full moon four times in five. Home court is worth about {{home_typical}} points. A moon effect the size of home court could exist and this data would probably miss it.

The window estimate of {{window_net_plain}} points could be real too. Confirming an edge that size at the usual standard would take about {{seasons_needed}} seasons of games in all, which puts the answer somewhere around {{answer_year}}.

There is a cheaper test than waiting. The source has {{second_seasons}} earlier seasons, {{second_first}} through {{second_last}}, that nothing above touched: {{second_games}} games, {{second_wolves_games}} of them the Wolves'. I ran the same model on them, unchanged, and asked first about the two estimates that stood out. The {{second_local_seasons}} oldest of those seasons record tip-off in the home arena's local time, which I converted first.

The league-wide distance effect did not come back. In the second sample it is {{second_dist}} points ({{second_dist_lo}} to {{second_dist_hi}}). The Wolves' defense inside the window kept its direction and lost most of its size. Opponents scored {{second_def_abs}} fewer points, {{second_def_ratio}}% of the first estimate, with an interval from {{second_def_lo}} to {{second_def_hi}}.

The record that started all this did worse. In those {{second_seasons}} seasons the Wolves went {{second_near_record}} near a full moon and {{second_far_record}} otherwise, which is {{second_near_pct}}% and {{second_far_pct}}%.

The Wolves' estimate keeps its shape under {{other_versions}} other versions of the model: regular season only, without the two pandemic seasons, recent seasons only, points per 48 minutes in place of an overtime term, and two simpler structures. The margin swing ranges from {{sens_min}} to {{sens_max}} and every interval includes zero.

The width of the window is a choice as well. I tried {{window_widths}}, from half a day to three days either side of the full moon, and the margin estimate ran from {{window_min}} to {{window_max}} points. The two-day window is the only one whose interval excludes zero. I had set a day and a half before seeing any of them. The numbers are in results/windows.csv.

The run needs R with the packages lme4, suncalc, ggplot2, ragg and jsonlite. It downloads {{season_files}} season files, fits the models, redraws the figures and rebuilds this page in six to ten minutes. The script fills in every result on this page from its own output. What changed from earlier drafts

The first versions of this analysis tested the moon by sliding the lunar calendar forward a random number of days and refitting. I removed that test. A cosine slid by any amount is a mix of the same cosine and sine, so the test's answer depended on where in the month the effect peaked and ignored how big it was. In a check, an effect twenty times larger barely moved the p-value. The fake moons replaced it.

Those versions also treated each team as equally good for all ten seasons and measured only the Wolves' scoring. This one lets team quality change every season and measures defense as well. The full list is in the changelog.
